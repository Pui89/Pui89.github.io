# PUI89 Commercial Foundation

**Status:** Strategy and governance baseline — proposed operating policy, not evidence of incorporation, revenue, customer contracts, certifications, or product readiness.

## 1. Company positioning

**PUI89 — Applied AI, Intelligent Systems & Robotics**

PUI89 is an independent, pre-incorporation technology initiative developing evidence-led AI software and simulation-first intelligent-system concepts for agriculture, disaster response, screening support, home safety, and mobility.

**Promise:** Practical intelligence, measurable evidence, responsible deployment.

PUI89 should sell outcomes and validated capabilities—not speculative autonomy, impressive renders, or model names.

## 2. Commercial operating model

Use a staged, low-capital model:

1. **Research and partner-funded pilots:** define a real customer problem, scope a measurable pilot, and seek a university, public-sector, industry, or grant partner.
2. **Software-first products:** build small, demonstrable tools with documented inputs, outputs, limits, deployment requirements, and support boundaries.
3. **Licensing and implementation:** consider paid software licensing, customization, integration, training, and support only after ownership, licence compatibility, security, and customer value are established.
4. **Hardware partnerships:** prototype physical systems with qualified engineering partners; do not promise manufacturing or field deployment before safety, reliability, environmental, and regulatory validation.

No revenue, customer, partner, certification, or commercial deployment may be claimed unless documented.

## 4. Who may pay, and for what

Validate buyers before building features:

- Agricultural cooperatives, plantations, equipment providers and research institutions: workflow assessment, crop-data preparation, harvest decision-support pilots.
- Property operators, building managers and home-technology integrators: monitoring workflow prototypes and evidence reporting.
- Disaster-response agencies, universities and NGOs: simulation, scenario analysis and research collaboration.
- Screening-system researchers and authorized operators: evaluation tools and human-review workflow research, subject to applicable law and data governance.
- Mobility and robotics companies/universities: simulation, requirements engineering and research tooling.

Do not imply endorsement by any named organization without written permission.

## 5. Revenue hypotheses to test

Potential models, only after validation:

- Paid feasibility study or scoped pilot.
- Software licence or annual institutional licence.
- Integration and customization services.
- Dataset preparation and evaluation services where data rights permit.
- Research grants, sponsored development, and institutional collaboration.
- Hardware licensing or joint development only after IP and safety review.

Do not publish prices until discovery interviews establish buyer, budget owner, procurement route, deployment cost, support burden, and measurable value. Never promise a grant or future revenue.

## 6. Product discovery standard

Before a product build is approved, record:

- Named user and buyer (they may differ).
- Painful, frequent and costly problem.
- Existing workaround and competing alternatives.
- Required data, consent, ownership and lawful basis.
- Minimum useful outcome and measurable success metric.
- Deployment environment, connectivity and hardware constraints.
- Safety, security, privacy and regulatory risks.
- Cost to build, operate, maintain and support.
- Pilot partner or credible route to recruit test users.
- Stop condition if evidence or economics fail.

Conduct structured interviews; record date, role, consent, anonymized notes, problem frequency, current cost, willingness to pilot, and next action. Do not treat compliments or website visits as demand.

## 7. Commercial discipline

- No paid service, subscription, cloud GPU, API, or model download without explicit cost approval.
- Keep a zero-cost baseline and record any unavoidable operating cost before adoption.
- Use open-source components only after checking exact licence, model/data terms, provenance, security, export/use restrictions and attribution obligations.
- Keep customer data out of public repositories and model-training workflows unless rights and consent are explicit.
- Define support hours, service limits, data retention, incident handling and version policy before taking money.
- Separate company, personal and public-sector assets; obtain legal advice before transferring employer-created work or using non-public government data.
- Review incorporation, tax, contracts, IP ownership, privacy and product-liability requirements with qualified Thai professionals before commercial contracting.

## 8. First 90-day execution sequence

### Days 1–15: foundation
- Select one narrow buyer/problem for the first pilot.
- Create a product one-page brief and problem interview script.
- Inventory code, datasets, licences, contributors and third-party assets.
- Establish a public claim register: concept / prototype / tested / externally validated / deployable.

### Days 16–45: evidence
- Build one small software workflow around existing code.
- Add reproducible tests, sample data with clear rights, and a limitations page.
- Record baseline metrics and failure cases.
- Interview potential users and document evidence, not assumptions.

### Days 46–75: pilot design
- Prepare a scoped pilot plan with deliverables, exclusions, success criteria, data handling, responsibilities and cost assumptions.
- Seek a qualified research or industry collaborator.
- Conduct a security, safety, licence and privacy review.

### Days 76–90: go/no-go
- Demonstrate the workflow using lawful sample data.
- Publish a concise evidence report.
- Decide whether to continue, pivot, partner, or stop based on user evidence, performance, cost and risk.
- Do not announce a commercial product unless release gates are met.

## 9. Commercial decision rule

A project advances only when all are true:

1. A real user problem and plausible buyer are evidenced.
2. The team can legally use the code, data and models.
3. The prototype solves a narrow task measurably better than a stated baseline.
4. Failure modes and limitations are documented.
5. Safety, security, privacy and deployment risks have owners and mitigations.
6. Delivery and support costs are understood.
7. A pilot can be conducted responsibly.

Otherwise it remains research, concept work, or is paused.

## 10. Public communication

Use precise status labels: **Concept**, **In development**, **Prototype**, **Tested in defined conditions**, **Externally validated**, **Available for pilot**, **Commercially released**.

Never use “production-ready”, “autonomous”, “certified”, “waterproof”, “fire-resistant”, “field-proven”, or “commercially available” without relevant evidence and approvals.
